9.1Stress and strain

Stress is the force exerted per unit area; strain is the physical change that results. When applied stress is greater than the internal strength of a rock, strain shows up as deformation: a change in the rock's volume and/or shape, or fracturing. There are three types of stress (Figure 9.2):

  • : forces pull in opposite directions; the strain stretches and thins the rock.
  • : forces push together; the strain shows as folding and thickening.
  • : transverse forces; blocks or regions of rock move past each other.

When rocks are stressed, the strain can be elastic, ductile, or brittle; the general name for this change is .

  • is reversible once the stress is released, like a stretched rubber band snapping back.
  • is permanent: the material can no longer return to its original shape, like a metal bar bent too far. The point where elastic deformation is passed and strain becomes permanent is the .
  • is another point of no return: the rock loses its integrity and fractures under increasing stress.

Which kind of deformation a rock undergoes depends on pore pressure, strain rate, rock strength, temperature, stress intensity, time, and confining pressure:

  • : pressure exerted on the rock by fluids in the open spaces (pores) within rock or sediment.
  • : how quickly a material is deformed. Stress applied slowly makes it easier to bend a piece of wood without breaking it.

9.3.2 Strike and dip

Strike and dip describe the orientation of rock layers relative to north–south and to horizontal (Figure 9.4).

  • : picture standing water on an inclined layer. The waterline is horizontal and points in a compass direction at some angle to true north or south; that angle, measured with a special compass, is the strike. N 30° E (read "north 30 degrees east") means the horizontal line points northeast, 30° from true north.
  • : the angle a bed plunges into the Earth from horizontal, plus the direction it goes down, such as 45° SE. The dip direction is the way a ball would roll if set on the layer and released. A horizontal bed dips 0°; a vertical bed dips 90°.
  • Strike and dip together are the rock's . On a map the symbol looks like a capital T with an extra-wide top and a short trunk: the top line is drawn at the strike angle from true north, the trunk points down-dip, and the number beside it is the dip angle.

9.4.1 Anticline

Anticlines are arch-like, A-shaped folds that are convex upward. Their limbs curve down, and the beds dip down and away from the fold axis. The oldest strata are in the center, along the axis, and younger beds are on the outside. Because a geologic map shows where surface topography cuts the structures below, an anticline can be recognized on a map by the attitude of its strata and by the older rocks inside the fold (Figure 9.7). An antiform has the same shape, but the relative ages of its beds cannot be determined. Oil geologists care about anticlines because they can form oil traps: oil migrates up the limbs and collects at the high point along the axis.

9.4.2 Syncline

Synclines are trough-like, U-shaped folds that are concave upward. Their beds dip down and in toward the fold axis. Older rock is on the outside and the youngest rock is inside, along the axis. A synform has a syncline's shape but, like an antiform, no distinguishable age zones. The chapter shows a 3D model you can rotate: Synclinal fold, Macigno Formation (Sketchfab, by alanpitts) ↗.

9.4.3 Monocline

Monoclines are step-like folds: flat rocks are warped up or down, then continue flat. They are common on the Colorado Plateau, where they form "reefs": ridges that act as topographic barriers (not ocean reefs; Unit 5). Capitol Reef in Utah is a monocline. Monoclines can form when shallow sedimentary strata bend over faults growing below them. These are called because they end before reaching the surface; they can be normal or reverse faults.

9.4.4 Dome

A dome is a symmetrical to semi-symmetrical upwarping of rock beds, shaped like an inverted bowl or an architectural dome. Utah examples are the San Rafael Swell, Harrisburg Junction Dome, and the Henry Mountains. Domes form by compression, by igneous intrusions underneath (Unit 4), by salt diapirs, or even by impacts, as with Upheaval Dome in Canyonlands National Park.

9.4.5 Basin

A basin is the inverse of a dome: a bowl-shaped depression in rock beds. The Uinta Basin in Utah is an example. Some structural basins are also that collect large amounts of sediment over time. They can form by folding but more commonly form during mountain building, between mountain blocks or by faulting. As a basin sinks (), it collects more sediment, and the weight of that sediment causes more subsidence: a positive-feedback loop. The rapidly subsiding Oquirrh Basin of Pennsylvanian–Permian age collected over 9,144 m (30,000 ft) of fossil-rich sandstone, shale, and limestone, now seen on Mt. Timpanogos and in Provo Canyon.

9.5Faults

are places in the crust where brittle deformation occurs as two blocks of rock move relative to each other. means rupture of rocks; it happens at plate boundaries and also within plates. Faults slip along the .

  • Normal and reverse faults have vertical, or , motion: relative up-and-down movement along a dipping fault between two blocks.
  • The is the block above the fault plane; the is below it. Picture a mine tunnel along a fault: a miner hangs a lantern on the hanging wall, and the footwall is at the miner's feet.
  • The is the step in the land surface where the fault breaks through.
  • are polished, often grooved surfaces on the fault plane, made by friction during movement.
  • A (fracture) is a plane of brittle deformation with no offset or shearing. Joints form by cooling, depressurizing, or folding, and joint systems can affect many square miles.

9.5.1 Normal faults

In the hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall, along the dip of the fault. They are made by tensional forces and are common at divergent plate boundaries, where the crust is stretched (Unit 2). Utah examples: the Wasatch Fault, the Hurricane Fault, and the faults bounding the valleys of the Basin and Range Province.

, , and are blocks of crust bounded by normal faults (Figure 9.13). Grabens drop down relative to the blocks beside them and make valleys; horsts rise relative to the dropped blocks and make higher ground. Together they give a symmetrical pattern of valleys and mountains with normal faults on both sides. A half-graben is the one-sided version: a block tilted by a normal fault on one side only, giving an asymmetrical valley and mountain. The Basin and Range Province of western Utah and Nevada is a series of full and half-grabens from the Salt Lake Valley to the Sierra Nevada.

Normal faults do not continue down into the mantle. In the Basin and Range, a normal fault's dip tends to decrease with depth: the fault gets shallower and more horizontal as it goes down. This happens where large amounts of extension take place on very low-angle normal faults called (Figure 9.14).

9.5.2 Reverse faults

In , compressional forces push the hanging wall up relative to the footwall (Figure 9.15). A is a reverse fault whose plane dips at a low angle, less than 45°. Thrust faults carry older rocks on top of younger rocks and can even repeat rock units in the stratigraphic record.

At subduction zones on convergent plate boundaries, denser oceanic crust is driven beneath less dense crust along a special kind of reverse fault, the . Megathrusts cause the largest earthquakes yet measured, and often massive destruction and tsunamis.

Thrust sheets have their own terms (Figure 9.16). The overriding sheet is the (allochthon), and the rock below the thrust is the autochthon. A is a remnant of the hanging wall left after the surrounding material has eroded away. A is where part of the hanging wall has eroded away to expose the footwall. The map symbol for a thrust puts triangular flags (teeth) on the overlying plate.

9.5.3 Strike-slip faults

have side-to-side motion. They are most often found at transform plate boundaries and are common in transform fracture zones along mid-ocean ridges. In pure strike-slip motion the blocks do not move up or down relative to each other, only sideways. The sense of movement is named by an observer standing on one block: if the block across the fault moves to the left, the motion is (left-lateral); if it moves to the right, it is (right-lateral) (Figure 9.18). The rule works from either side of the fault.

Bends in strike-slip faults create areas of compression or tension between the sliding blocks (Unit 2). Tension creates features with normal faults and basins, such as the Salton Sea in California. Compression creates features with reverse faults and small-scale mountain building, such as the San Gabriel Mountains in California. The faults that splay off these features are called (Figure 9.19).

The San Andreas Fault, the transform boundary between the North American and Pacific plates, is a dextral (right-lateral) strike-slip fault. The Dead Sea fault in Jordan and Israel is sinistral (left-lateral).

9.6Earthquake essentials

Earthquakes are felt at Earth's surface when energy is released by blocks of rock sliding past each other, that is, when faulting has occurred. The released seismic energy travels through the Earth as seismic waves. Most earthquakes happen along active plate boundaries. , away from plate boundaries, also happen and are still poorly understood. See the USGS real-time earthquake map ↗.

9.6.1 How earthquakes happen

The release of seismic energy is explained by (Figure 9.20). The blocks of long-lived faults such as the Wasatch Fault, which move again and again, are locked together by friction. Over hundreds to thousands of years stress builds along the fault until it overcomes the friction, ruptures the rock, and starts the fault moving. The deformed but unbroken rocks then snap back toward their original shape: . Think of bending a stick until it breaks: stored energy is released and the pieces return to near their original orientation.

  • The bending of rocks near a fault shows stress building up. In earthquake-prone areas such as California, strain gauges measure this bending to help , scientists who study earthquakes, learn more about predicting them.
  • Where a fault is not locked, stress causes slow, continuous displacement called , as on parts of the San Andreas Fault.
  • After an earthquake, stress keeps acting and elastic energy builds again during a quiet period. Some of it may be released as small earthquakes on or near the main fault called , hours or days before a large earthquake, or not at all. The main release of energy is the . may follow as the rock adjusts to new strain, and they generally decrease over time.

9.6.2 Focus and epicenter

The earthquake , also called the hypocenter, is the point on the fault plane where rupture starts and from which the first seismic waves spread out; it is always some depth below the surface. From the focus, displacement spreads up, down, and sideways along the fault plane and produces seismic waves. The larger the displacement and the farther it spreads along the fault, the more energy is released and the stronger and longer the shaking. The is the point on the surface directly above the focus. News reports give the epicenter because it is the center of the area where people are affected.

9.6.3 Seismic waves

Waves carry energy through a medium, such as rock or loose sediment. Their basic properties (Figure 9.22):

  • : the height of the motion; for earthquakes, the size of the ground motion.
  • : the distance between two successive peaks.
  • : the number of repetitions of the motion per unit of time (cycles per time unit).
  • : the time for a wave to travel one wavelength; it is the inverse of frequency.

When waves combine they interfere (Figure 9.23). Waves in sync give : one adds to and magnifies the other. Waves out of sync give , which shrinks both. Two waves of the same amplitude and frequency that are half a wavelength out of sync can cancel each other completely. Amplitude, frequency, period, and interference together shape the magnitude and intensity of earthquakes.

Seismic waves are the physical expression of the energy released by elastic rebound, felt as an earthquake. They come as body waves and surface waves.

pass underground through Earth's interior and are the first waves to spread out from the focus:

  • (primary) are the fastest. They move through rock by compression, much as sound moves through air: rock particles move forward and back along the direction of travel, so P waves can pass through solids, liquids, plasma, and gases.
  • (secondary) are slower and follow the P waves. They are shear waves that move particles side to side, so they travel only through solids, not liquids, plasma, or gases.

Body waves spread through the Earth as a roughly spherical wave front. The path followed by one point on the spreading front is a , which reaches a seismograph at one of thousands of monitoring stations. Density increases with depth, and seismic velocity increases with it, so bends rays away from the vertical and back up toward the surface.

form when body waves from the focus strike Earth's surface; they travel along the surface outward from the epicenter, mostly from S-wave energy with some from P waves (Figure 9.26). They are slower than body waves, and their complex horizontal and vertical motion causes most of an earthquake's damage.

  • shake the ground mainly side to side (horizontally) and are the most destructive, despite their name.
  • roll the ground in an elliptical motion with lengthwise compression and expansion, like ocean waves, but particles move in the opposite direction to water particles in ocean waves.

9.6.4 Induced seismicity

means earthquakes caused by human activity, for example near natural gas extraction sites. Waste fluids are injected into the ground, commonly a byproduct of , an extraction process for natural gas. The fluid raises the pore pressure (the outward pressure of liquid in a rock's pores), which lowers the friction that keeps rocks from sliding past each other, in effect lubricating fault planes. The big increase in drilling in the central United States has produced large volumes of waste drilling fluid to dispose of, and a measurable rise in the number of earthquakes in the region (Figure 9.28).

9.7Measuring earthquakes

9.7.1 Seismographs

People feel about 1 million earthquakes a year, usually when they are close to the source and the earthquake registers at least moment magnitude 2.5. Major earthquakes of magnitude 7.0 and higher are extremely rare.

measure even the slightest ground vibrations (Figure 9.29). Early 20th-century instruments used a weighted pen (a pendulum) hung from a long spring above a recording device fixed solidly to the ground: a rotating drum wrapped in a continuous strip of paper. During an earthquake the hanging pen stays still and records the ground's movement on the paper. The resulting graph is a . Digital versions use magnets, wire coils, electrical sensors, and digital signals instead of pens, springs, drums, and paper. A is a set of seismographs that measure vibration in three directions: north–south (x), east–west (y), and up–down (z).

To locate an epicenter, seismologists use the different arrival times of the waves (Figure 9.30). P waves appear first on a seismogram, then S waves, and finally surface waves, which have the largest amplitude. Surface waves lose energy quickly, so they are not measurable far from the epicenter. The time differences give the distance to the epicenter but not its direction.

With arrival times from several stations, seismologists use to pinpoint the epicenter (Figure 9.31). At least three stations are needed. The distance from each station is plotted as the radius of a circle, and the epicenter is where the circles meet. The same method works in 3D with multi-axis seismographs and spheres instead of circles, which gives the depth of the focus.

9.7.2 Seismograph network

The International Registry of Seismograph Stations ↗ lists more than 20,000 seismographs. By comparing data from many of them, scientists map the inside of the Earth, detect large explosions, and predict tsunamis. The Global Seismographic Network ↗, a worldwide set of linked seismographs that share real-time data, has more than 150 stations that meet set design and precision standards. USArray is a network of hundreds of permanent and transportable seismographs used to map the subsurface of the United States. Besides earthquakes and related hazards, the global network helps detect nuclear weapons tests, which are monitored by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization; seismographs recently recorded nuclear tests by North Korea.

9.7.3 Seismic tomography

Just as a CT (computed tomography) scan uses X-rays at many angles to image the inside of a body, uses the seismic rays of the thousands of earthquakes each year, passing at all angles through rock, to image structures inside the Earth.

Assuming the Earth is made of uniform layers, geologists built a model of the expected properties of Earth materials at every depth: the (Preliminary Reference Earth Model). These properties include seismic wave velocity, which depends on rock density and elasticity. In the mantle, temperature changes density: cooler rock is denser and transmits seismic waves faster; warmer rock is less dense and transmits them slower. Differences between the measured arrival times at seismic stations and the times predicted by PREM are . Because rays from many earthquakes cross rock bodies at all angles and reach many stations, these differences can be built into 3D images of the Earth's interior.

Seismologists have imaged the Farallon Plate, which subducted beneath North America, and the Yellowstone magma chamber, a product of the Yellowstone hotspot. Peculiarities of Farallon subduction are thought to explain many features of western North America, including the Rocky Mountains (Unit 8).

9.7.4 Earthquake magnitude and intensity

measures the energy released by an earthquake. rates how strongly the ground shakes and what damage results at a given place.

Richter scale. The (ML), the first and best-known magnitude scale, was developed by Charles F. Richter (1900–1985) at the California Institute of Technology. Richter magnitude comes from the maximum amplitude of the pen trace on the seismogram, adjusted for distance using the S–P arrival-time difference. The scale is , based on powers of 10: each step of one unit is a 10-fold increase in seismic-wave amplitude (a magnitude 6 shakes the ground 10 times more than a magnitude 5), but about 32 times more energy. It was built for Southern California earthquakes using local seismographs, so it works poorly for long distances and very large earthquakes, and most agencies no longer use it. News media often still report Richter magnitudes right after an earthquake.

Moment magnitude scale. The (MW) gives the absolute size of an earthquake by combining information from many locations. It is calculated from the actual energy released, using the area of the rupture, the amount of slip, and the rigidity of the rocks. Because each earthquake happens in its own geologic setting and the rupture area is often hard to measure, it can take days or even months to calculate. It is also logarithmic, and its values are about equal to Richter values except for very large earthquakes. It is more accurate and more useful for science.

Modified Mercalli Intensity scale. The (MMI) is a qualitative rating of shaking based on observed damage and people's perceptions, from I (lowest) to X (highest) in the chapter's table (Table 9.7). Intensity varies with distance from the epicenter and with population density, for example urban versus rural settings. Scientists used it to classify earthquakes before quantitative magnitudes existed. Intensity maps show where damage was most severe, based on questionnaires, local news, and on-site reports.

9.8Earthquake risk

9.8.1 Factors that determine shaking

Magnitude is an absolute value for the energy released. Intensity, how much the ground shakes, depends on several factors:

  • Magnitude: in general, the larger the magnitude, the stronger and longer the shaking (Table 9.8).
  • Location and direction: shaking is stronger closer to the epicenter, and depends on where the observer is relative to the epicenter, the direction the rupture spreads, and the path of greatest rupture.
  • Local geologic conditions: different ground materials respond differently, like a block of Jello versus a meatloaf hit by the same wave. Solid bedrock shakes less than unconsolidated sediment. Seismic waves move fastest through consolidated bedrock, slower through unconsolidated sediment, and slowest through unconsolidated sediment with a lot of water. When waves slow down, energy is transferred to amplitude, so surface motion and shaking grow ().
  • Focus depth: deeper earthquakes shake the surface less, because much of their energy, carried by body waves, is lost before reaching the surface, where surface waves are made.

9.8.2 Factors that determine destruction

  • Building materials: how flexible a material is decides how well it resists shaking. (URM) suffers the most (Figure 9.35). Wood framing fastened with nails bends and flexes and is more likely to survive; steel can also deform elastically before it fails. Salt Lake City's Fix the Bricks ↗ campaign explains URM safety.
  • Intensity and duration: stronger and longer shaking destroys more.
  • : when the frequency of seismic waves matches a building's natural frequency of shaking, the shaking grows. In the 1985 Mexico City earthquake, buildings 6 to 15 stories tall were especially vulnerable. Earthquake-resilient skyscrapers have dampers and to reduce resonance. Resonance depends on the building's materials, so a change in a building's measured resonance can signal a change in its structural integrity.

9.8.3 Earthquake recurrence

A long pause in activity on a fault segment with a history of repeated earthquakes is a . The quiet may mean the segment is locked and building strain, which raises the chance of another earthquake. Geologists dig across faults to estimate how often past earthquakes happened (Figure 9.36). Trenches work best for faults with long , roughly hundreds to tens of thousands of years between significant earthquakes; places with frequent earthquakes usually have more recorded data instead.

9.8.5 Secondary hazards caused by earthquakes

Most damage comes from shaking and fault displacement, but secondary hazards endanger people and structures too, sometimes after the shaking stops.

: water-saturated, unconsolidated sediments, usually silt or sand, become fluid-like when shaken. Shaking breaks the cohesion between grains, making a slurry of particles suspended in water. Buildings settle or tilt into the liquefied sediment, which looks like movie quicksand (Figure 9.38). Liquefaction also makes , cones formed when liquefied sand squirts up through an overlying, usually finer-grained layer.

Watch: Liquefaction demonstrated ↗ · Liquefaction in Makuhari, 2011 ↗

are earthquake-induced ocean waves, among the most devastating natural disasters. When fault movement or an underwater landslide offsets the sea floor, the displacement lifts a volume of ocean water and starts the wave. In deep water tsunamis are fast with low amplitude; in shallower water near shore they slow down and grow much taller (Figure 9.39). Just before one strikes, the trough ahead of the crest draws the water far back from shore. Curious people who follow the retreating water can be caught by an oncoming wall of water that may be 30 m (100 ft) high. Early warning systems save lives. Ocean waves, including tsunamis, are covered in Chapter 12.

Landslides: shaking can trigger landslides (Chapter 10). The 1992 St. George, Utah, earthquake set off a landslide that destroyed several structures in the Balanced Rock Hills subdivision (Figure 9.40).

are waves made in lakes by earthquakes: shaking sloshes the water back and forth or changes the lake's depth. Seiches in Hebgen Lake during a 1959 earthquake caused major damage to nearby structures and roads. Video: a seiche in a swimming pool, Nepal 2015 ↗

Land elevation changes: elastic rebound and fault displacement can lower (subsidence) or raise (upheaval) the land. In the 1964 Alaska earthquake, the height difference between hanging wall and footwall ranged from one to several meters (3–30 ft). The Wasatch Mountains are an accumulation of fault scarps made a few meters at a time over a few million years.

Summary

Stress (applied force) comes in three types: tension, compression, and shear. It produces strain, which can be elastic, ductile, or brittle. Geologic maps show surface formations, the surface expression of 3D structures below; the strike-and-dip symbol gives the orientation of strata. Folds are classified by their limbs, axes, and axial planes. Faults form when stress exceeds the rock's strength and friction; the three major types are normal, reverse, and strike-slip. Earthquakes are sudden brittle failure with elastic rebound, sending out body waves (P and S) and surface waves (Love and Rayleigh). Seismographs record them; triangulation locates the epicenter; moment magnitude has replaced the Richter scale for size, and Mercalli intensity rates the shaking. Damage depends on magnitude, location, ground, building materials, duration, and resonance, and secondary hazards add liquefaction, tsunamis, landslides, seiches, and elevation changes.

Quick recap

  • Tension → stretching, normal faults; compression → shortening, folds, reverse and thrust faults; shear → tearing, strike-slip faults.
  • Elastic (reversible) → yield point → ductile (permanent) → brittle failure. Heat makes rock more ductile; fast strain and strong rock make it more brittle.
  • Strike = direction of a horizontal line on the bed; dip = angle and direction down from horizontal.
  • Anticline/dome: oldest in the center. Syncline/basin: youngest in the center.
  • Normal: hanging wall down. Reverse: hanging wall up (thrust under 45°). Strike-slip: sinistral or dextral.
  • Focus (hypocenter) underground; epicenter above it. P waves first, then S, then surface waves (largest).
  • S waves cannot cross the liquid outer core → shadow zones.
  • Magnitude = energy (×10 amplitude, ×32 energy per unit); intensity = shaking and damage (Mercalli).
